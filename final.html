Findings and How to Read the Dashboard

This section explains what each view is showing, what patterns matter, and how to use the charts as filters for the map.

Street cleaning is a city system that people experience one block at a time

This dashboard turns San Francisco street sweeping from a confusing set of parking signs into a citywide pattern. Public Works describes mechanical sweeping as covering most city streets and removing large amounts of litter and debris, while SFMTA frames street-cleaning citations as a way to keep sweepers from being blocked.

The main finding is that street cleaning is not evenly distributed. Frequency, time, and street type all matter: some groups dominate the citywide schedule, some weekday/time windows are much busier than others, and several major corridors stand out when full street names are totaled.

Spatial The map shows where high-frequency sweeping clusters.
Temporal The heatmap shows which weekdays and hours dominate.
Comparative The treemap and bars show which groups and streets stand out.

Outside context

Local reporting often frames street cleaning as both a cleanliness system and a parking headache, especially because signs vary block by block.

How to use it

Start with the map, then click a chart element to filter the city. Click a street segment when you want the exact schedule.

The map shows where sweeping intensity is concentrated

Each line is a street segment. Lighter blues mean lower estimated sweeps per month, while darker blues mean more frequent sweeping. The map makes the schedule feel less random: high-frequency streets often appear as visible corridors and dense clusters rather than isolated single blocks.

This helps explain why street cleaning feels different depending on where you park. Residential blocks often sit in lighter frequency groups, while busier grids and commercial corridors tend to show stronger activity. Use the map as the main view, then use the charts to explain the pattern.

Finding

The darkest streets are not evenly scattered. They tend to line up with denser street grids and major corridors.

Interaction

Click a street segment to see its limits, days cleaned, time window, frequency group, and full schedule summary.

The heatmap shows that timing is not evenly spread across the week

The heatmap groups scheduled sweeping by weekday and time window. Darker cells mean more estimated monthly scheduled occurrences. The clearest pattern is that weekday morning windows dominate, especially compared with weekends and later-day windows.

The play button animates those same weekday/time buckets across the map. It is not showing live sweeper locations; it is a rough scheduled-pattern animation that helps reveal how sweeping activity moves through the city over the week.

Finding

The strongest cells are mostly weekday morning periods. Weekend cells are much lighter, so the schedule is not balanced across all days.

Interaction

Click one cell, a weekday label, or a time label. Press play to animate each weekday/time window across the map.

The treemap shows which frequency groups define the system

Each rectangle is a sweeps-per-month group, and its area shows how many street segments fall into that group. The biggest groups in this dataset are the mid-frequency categories, especially 4x and 7–8x per month.

The smaller high-frequency groups are still important. They cover fewer segments, but they represent the most intensive schedules. So the treemap separates what is most common citywide from what is most intense locally.

Finding

The distribution is skewed. A few mid-frequency groups dominate, while the highest-frequency groups are smaller but more intense.

Interaction

Click a rectangle to highlight every street segment in that frequency group. Hover to see the segment count.

The bar chart identifies the streets with the most total scheduled activity

This chart groups segments by street name and totals their estimated monthly sweeping activity. Major corridors like Mission St, 03rd St, Market St, and Van Ness Ave stand out because they combine many mapped segments with repeated cleaning schedules.

This chart should not be read as "one block gets cleaned this many times." It is a grouped street-name total. A street can rank high because it is long, because its segments are swept often, or because both are true.

Finding

The top streets are mostly recognizable corridors, suggesting that sweeping intensity is tied to city structure rather than isolated blocks.

Interaction

Click a bar to highlight all mapped segments with that street name. Then use the map to see whether the activity is clustered or spread out.

Data & Methodology

How the Dashboard Was Built

This dashboard combines San Francisco street sweeping schedule records with street geometry to show where cleaning is scheduled, when it happens, and how individual streets compare to citywide patterns. The main challenge was turning raw schedule rows into street-level, time-level, and corridor-level summaries that could be explored interactively.

Data

The project uses San Francisco's Street Sweeping Schedule data and a street geometry dataset. The schedule data includes fields such as street name, limits, weekday, time range, week-of-month indicators, holiday status, side information, and CNN.

Join Key

CNN, or Centerline Network Number, was used to connect the schedule records to the mapped street segments. This made it possible to color actual street geometry by its cleaning schedule instead of only showing a table of rules.

Metric

The main map value is estimated sweeps per month. It is called estimated because it is derived from schedule fields, not from live sweeper vehicle data. Week indicators and schedule entries were aggregated by street segment to estimate typical monthly cleaning frequency.

Aggregation

Different views use different levels of aggregation. The map and selected-street panel use individual street segments, the heatmap aggregates scheduled occurrences by weekday and time window, and the top-streets chart groups segments by full street name.

Processing Pipeline

Raw schedule records were cleaned and grouped with Python generated with the help of Claude before being loaded into D3. The processed outputs included one street-segment detail file, a weekday/time heatmap table, a frequency-group table, and a top-streets summary. Preprocessing the data kept the browser dashboard faster and made the visualizations easier to link together.

Important Caveats

  • Estimated sweeps per month means scheduled frequency, not confirmed sweeper trips.
  • Heatmap values are aggregated scheduled occurrences, not unique street segments.
  • Top streets are full-street totals, so long streets can rank highly because they contain many mapped segments.
  • Street-side details are shown when available, but the main map still represents centerline street segments.
